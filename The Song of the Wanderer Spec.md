The Song of the Wanderer
## Full Game Design Specification

**Document status:** Foundational game specification  
**Target platform:** PC / Steam  
**Genre:** Narrative-driven historical travel RPG with light trading and relationship systems  
**Core promise:** Take the player on an extraordinary journey through the ancient world as it changes around them.

---

## 1. High concept

*The Song of the Wanderer* is a long-form, narrative-led adventure set across the interconnected worlds of the Late Bronze Age and the centuries of transformation that follow.

The player begins as a seemingly ordinary merchant. They travel between ports, cities, river settlements, courts, temples, caravan stops, and distant communities. They trade goods, learn languages, cultivate relationships, earn the trust of influential people, and become entangled in the lives of ordinary families and powerful rulers.

Then the world begins to change.

The political and commercial networks of the Late Bronze Age fracture. Cities are threatened, trade routes become dangerous, rulers lose control, people flee, and old centers of power disappear. The player cannot prevent history wholesale, but can change what happens to individuals, families, communities, records, and relationships. Refugees encountered in one region may reappear years later in another. A merchant helped early in the game may become a valuable ally—or a rival. A child encountered during a flight from a destroyed city may grow into an influential adult.

The protagonist's unusual nature allows the story to extend beyond the lifespan and historical moment of an ordinary person. Different peoples know the protagonist by different names. Some names appear to belong to gods, heroes, or figures from older traditions. Some characters recognize the protagonist despite never having met them—or claim to have known them under another name. The game never provides a simple, definitive explanation of the protagonist's identity. The possibility that the protagonist is a demigod is persistent, meaningful, and increasingly difficult to dismiss, but remains open to interpretation.

The journey extends beyond the eastern Mediterranean. The player can travel south through Nubia and the Red Sea world, east through Mesopotamia and the Iranian plateau into South Asia, and eventually farther east toward the civilizations of ancient China. These are not distant cameos. They are fully realized regions with their own histories, societies, characters, political interests, languages, and transformations.

The game is about the people who inhabit history—and the extraordinary continuity of one traveler moving through it.

### Design pillars

1. **The journey is the game.** The player should always have a reason to wonder what lies beyond the next destination.
2. **People make history personal.** Recurring characters, families, relationships, and consequences are more important than abstract historical exposition.
3. **The ancient world is colorful and specific.** Each region should feel culturally distinct, inhabited, and worth understanding.
4. **Trade gives travel purpose.** Commerce creates practical decisions, opportunities, relationships, and reasons to visit places. It is not a complex economic simulation.
5. **History changes around the player.** Major historical developments are authored and grounded; local human outcomes can respond to player choices.
6. **The protagonist is a mystery, not a power fantasy.** Their possible divine nature creates wonder and narrative continuity, not a combat or progression superpower.
7. **Curiosity is rewarded.** Language learning, exploration, observation, and relationships reveal optional stories, useful information, and new possibilities.
8. **The game respects historical uncertainty.** Documented facts, scholarly interpretations, and fictional storylines must be distinguishable in the development process and, where appropriate, in the in-game journal.

## 2. Player experience

The player should feel like a traveler who gradually becomes a familiar and consequential presence across a vast world.

At the beginning, the player is relatively unknown. They have limited money, a modest inventory, few reliable contacts, and only partial command of the languages they need. They rely on local guides, other merchants, interpreters, and hospitality.

Over time, they become known in different places for different reasons. They might be welcomed by a family in one town, trusted by a harbor official in another, regarded with suspicion by a palace, and treated as an old friend by a merchant encountered decades earlier. A king may grant them safe passage. A temple attendant may recognize an old name. A refugee family may remember that the player helped them escape.

The player is not expected to become a ruler or conqueror. Their influence comes from movement, knowledge, relationships, favors, trade, and the unusual continuity of their presence. Think along the lines of the game Caravaneer (although tonality, difficulty and the like will probably be different).

### Desired emotional range

The game should make room for:

- Wonder at unfamiliar landscapes, cities, rituals, crafts, foods, animals, and languages.
- Warmth from friendships, hospitality, family life, and recurring companions.
- Satisfaction from learning how a place works and becoming more capable within it.
- Tension from dangerous travel, political instability, war, and difficult choices.
- Grief when familiar places are lost or characters suffer.
- Hope when people rebuild, reunite, adapt, or establish new lives.
- Mystery through recurring symbols, names, dreams, and unexplained recognition.
- Quiet moments in which the player simply observes the ancient world.

The experience should not be relentlessly grim. The Bronze Age Collapse is a major historical backdrop, but the ancient world is not merely a succession of disasters. It is full of ordinary life, humor, beauty, ambition, celebration, craft, family, and opportunity.

In terms of tonality, think along the lines of Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey, or Gladiator (although Gladiator is not appropriate for this time period).
We want deep emotional threads, relationship building, attachment to cultures, and the concept of a place called home that is always changing and even if it is found, it is transitory and temporary (e.g., many places that could become a home are torn apart by war, famine, or other issues).

## 3. Historical setting and chronology

### 3.1 Historical scope

The opening takes place in the interconnected Late Bronze Age, provisionally around **1230 BCE**. The central historical arc covers the upheavals conventionally associated with the end of the Late Bronze Age in the eastern Mediterranean, followed by the emergence of new political and cultural arrangements.

The full game may extend over multiple generations and centuries. Its final date should be selected after the narrative structure and protagonist's supernatural longevity have been defined. A useful initial planning horizon is approximately **1230–900 BCE**, with the option to extend farther if the story genuinely benefits from it.

This is a narrative planning range, not a claim that every region experienced the same events at the same time. The Late Bronze Age crisis was uneven, multi-causal, and chronologically complex. Political disruption, migration, conflict, environmental pressures, trade disruption, and local developments did not occur as one synchronized event. The game must reflect this regional variation.

### 3.2 The wider world is not frozen in the Mediterranean

The game should show that other regions were undergoing their own developments, whether or not those developments were caused by or connected to events in the eastern Mediterranean.

Important parallel settings include:

- **South Asia:** The post-urban and regionalized landscapes following the decline of the Mature Indus or Harappan urban system; communities and settlement patterns in the northwest and north; the emergence and spread of new cultural forms, including Painted Grey Ware in parts of northern India around the beginning of the first millennium BCE; and the development of early Vedic traditions.
- **China:** The Late Shang world centered on Anyang, including royal authority, divination, bronze production, warfare, ritual, and the eventual transition from Shang to Zhou rule around the mid-eleventh century BCE.
- **Nubia and the Middle Nile:** The complex political and cultural landscape south of Egypt, including the legacy of Kerma and Egyptian imperial domination, local elites, trade, and changing relations with Egypt.
- **The Red Sea and lands associated with Punt:** Maritime and coastal networks involving incense, resins, ivory, ebony, gold, animal products, and other goods. Punt's precise location and political geography remain uncertain and should not be presented as settled fact.
- **Mesopotamia and the Iranian plateau:** Political change, regional powers, trade, and routes connecting the Near East to Central and South Asia.
- **The Aegean and Anatolia:** The collapse or transformation of palace-centered political systems, the movement of people, and the development of new regional arrangements.

These histories should be presented as histories in their own right. They are not simply background scenery for the eastern Mediterranean crisis.

### 3.3 Historical confidence categories

Every historically significant content item must be classified internally as one of the following:

- **Documented:** Supported directly by contemporary texts, inscriptions, archaeological evidence, or securely established historical records.
- **Strongly supported interpretation:** A scholarly reconstruction supported by multiple forms of evidence but not directly attested in every detail.
- **Plausible reconstruction:** Consistent with what is known, but not firmly demonstrated.
- **Fictional narrative:** Invented characters, conversations, personal events, or connective stories.
- **Mystery / deliberately unresolved:** Material whose ambiguity is intentional, especially concerning the protagonist.

The game should not make fictional dialogue appear to be a surviving historical quotation. The journal and codex can provide short contextual notes, while the development database should retain detailed sources and confidence assessments.

### 3.4 Historical event design

Major events are authored, not simulated. The game does not calculate the collapse from population, food production, military strength, or economic variables.

Instead, the narrative timeline schedules major developments and selects relevant local consequences. A player might hear a rumor before seeing its effects, encounter displaced people after an event, or arrive at a destination after its political situation has changed.

The same historical event can be experienced differently depending on where the player is, who they know, what they have learned, and which routes remain open.

## 4. The playable world

### 4.1 Geographic philosophy

The world map should be broad enough to make the ancient world feel genuinely expansive. It should support long-distance journeys without implying that every region was connected by a well-established direct trade route.

The map is a narrative and travel interface, not a realistic navigation simulator. It presents known destinations, possible routes, travel modes, estimated journey lengths, risk, and relevant contextual information.

Routes may be:

- Well-established commercial or political routes.
- Regional routes used by local traders and travelers.
- Seasonal or conditional routes.
- Difficult routes that require guides, contacts, supplies, or special circumstances.
- Speculative or fictionalized connective routes, clearly treated as such in the historical design notes.

Long-distance movement into South Asia and China should be possible through a chain of regional routes and intermediaries. The player should not simply sail from the Levant to China on a direct, historically unsupported route.

### 4.2 Core geographic regions

#### A. Aegean and Mycenaean world

Potential destinations include Mycenaean centers, island communities, and coastal settlements. This region should show palace-centered societies and their disruption, followed by local adaptation and changing patterns of settlement and exchange.

Possible experiences:
- Palace officials and local elites.
- Bronze workers, shipbuilders, farmers, and traders.
- Religious festivals and local cult practices.
- Political uncertainty and the movement of skilled craftspeople.
- Communities adapting to the weakening or disappearance of palace institutions.

#### B. Anatolia and Cyprus

Potential destinations include Hittite territories and former centers of Hittite power, coastal communities, and Cyprus.

Cyprus should be a major commercial and cultural crossroads. It can serve as a natural transition between the Aegean, Levant, Anatolia, and wider maritime networks.

Possible experiences:
- Copper extraction, smelting, and trade.
- Merchant houses and harbor communities.
- Local rulers and competing external interests.
- Refugees and craftspeople moving between islands and mainland.
- The consequences of political disruption for trade and settlement.

#### C. Levant and eastern Mediterranean coast

Potential destinations include Ugarit, other coastal cities, inland settlements, and communities in Canaan.

Ugarit is an ideal opening location because it offers a rich commercial and diplomatic setting, a port, a palace, temples, scribal culture, and a well-documented historical association with the end of the Late Bronze Age.

Possible experiences:
- International merchants and interpreters.
- Palace correspondence and political intrigue.
- Scribes, archives, and the preservation or loss of records.
- Sailors, ship captains, artisans, and religious specialists.
- The gradual disruption of trade and the threat to coastal settlements.

The player may witness Ugarit's final crisis, but the game should not reduce its destruction to one simplistic cause or a single definitive explanation.

#### D. Egypt and the Nile

Egypt should be a major region with multiple destinations rather than one generic capital.

Possible locations and settings:
- Major administrative and religious centers.
- Nile towns and agricultural settlements.
- Delta ports and Mediterranean connections.
- Military and frontier communities.
- Southern Nile routes toward Nubia.

Possible experiences:
- Royal administration and court access.
- Priests, scribes, soldiers, craftspeople, and farmers.
- Temple festivals and ritual life.
- Diplomatic and commercial relationships.
- The contrast between royal ideology and ordinary life.
- Changing imperial priorities and relations with neighboring peoples.

The player may gain access to powerful figures, but court favor should be earned through relationships, service, language, reputation, and political judgment—not a single generic approval meter.

#### E. Nubia and the Middle Nile

This region is essential to the southern expansion of the game.

Potential destinations should include historically appropriate Nile settlements, trading centers, and communities farther south. Kerma and other Nubian cultural and political centers should be researched carefully for the selected dates. The game must distinguish the earlier Kerma kingdom from later political formations and avoid casually projecting the later Napatan or Meroitic kingdoms backward into this period.

Possible experiences:
- Nile trade and transport.
- Gold, livestock, ivory, animal products, and other regional goods.
- Local elites, officials, craftspeople, and pastoral communities.
- The legacy and consequences of Egyptian imperial control.
- Different forms of political authority and local identity.
- Travel farther south into regions less familiar to Mediterranean characters.

#### F. Red Sea and lands associated with Punt

The Red Sea should be a meaningful travel corridor, not merely a decorative edge of the map.

Potential destinations may include coastal settlements, ports, and regions associated with Egyptian expeditions and trade with Punt. Because Punt's precise location and boundaries are uncertain, the game should avoid presenting one exact location or a fully known state structure as fact.

Possible experiences:
- Maritime expeditions and coastal travel.
- Incense, resins, aromatic woods, ivory, and animal products.
- Local guides and trading communities.
- Difficult water and provisioning conditions.
- Different understandings of the same goods, places, and religious practices.

#### G. Mesopotamia

Potential destinations include major cities and regional centers in Babylonia, Assyria, and surrounding areas, selected according to the date and political situation.

Possible experiences:
- Urban commercial life and scribal institutions.
- Temple economies and religious traditions.
- Palace politics, diplomacy, and military service.
- Merchants moving goods between the Mediterranean, Gulf, and Iranian plateau.
- The changing political balance between regional powers.

#### H. Iranian plateau and Central Asian corridors

This region provides the overland bridge toward South Asia.

Potential destinations and routes should be selected through detailed historical and archaeological research. The game should not imply a single unified Iranian empire at this date. It should represent a patchwork of regions, settlements, local powers, pastoral communities, and routes.

Possible experiences:
- Caravan travel and mountain passes.
- Pack animals and local guides.
- Metals, stones, textiles, livestock, and other regional goods.
- Communities with different languages and customs.
- Seasonal travel constraints and difficult terrain.
- Merchants and intermediaries who connect distant commercial networks.

#### I. South Asia

South Asia must be a fully playable region, not merely a distant reference.

The player should be able to reach multiple distinct cultural and ecological zones, including northwestern regions associated with the post-urban Indus world and regions farther east where new settlement patterns and cultural forms were developing.

Possible experiences:
- Communities living among the material and urban legacies of the Indus civilization.
- Regional settlements, farmers, pastoralists, craftspeople, and traders.
- River systems and changing settlement patterns.
- Early Vedic ritual and oral traditions, represented carefully and without treating later texts as transparent records of every earlier practice.
- Regional languages and local forms of authority.
- Different understandings of kingship, ritual, ancestry, and the divine.

The game should not portray India as a single kingdom or unified civilization. It should reflect the diversity of the subcontinent and the uneven development of its regions.

#### J. Ancient China

China should also be a fully playable destination, with a strong focus on the Late Shang world and the transition toward Zhou rule.

A major destination should be the Anyang/Yinxu world, where the player can encounter:
- Royal authority and court politics.
- Oracle-bone divination and ritual specialists.
- Bronze production and elite exchange.
- Military campaigns and frontier relations.
- Artisans, laborers, merchants, and ordinary households.
- The political and ideological transition associated with the Shang-Zhou change.

The player may encounter the transition from Shang to Zhou as a major regional historical event. It should be portrayed through local perspectives and not as a simplistic morality tale.

Further eastern destinations may be included where historical evidence supports them and where they serve the narrative. The game should not pretend that the protagonist can freely traverse all of later imperial China in this period.

### 4.3 Geography and historical connectivity

The map should show the player what is known and what is uncertain.

For long-distance travel:
- Routes should be built as connected stages.
- The player may need to find a guide, interpreter, caravan, ship, or merchant contact for the next stage.
- Some routes may be unavailable until the player has established relationships or learned enough about the region.
- A route's existence in the game does not automatically mean it was a formal, continuous trade road.
- Distant regions should be connected through regional exchange, intermediaries, and plausible travel chains.
- The player should encounter the limits of their knowledge. Rumors about distant places may be incomplete or inaccurate.

The design should avoid anachronistically labeling the network a fully developed Silk Road. It is a web of older regional connections, exchange corridors, and human intermediaries.

### 4.4 Destination structure

Destinations are presented through illustrated location screens, not walkable city environments.

Each destination may offer a subset of activities:
- Marketplace
- Harbor, river landing, or caravanserai-like stopping place where historically appropriate
- Palace, administrative compound, or local ruler's residence
- Temple, shrine, or ritual space
- Residential quarter
- Workshop or craft district
- Tavern, inn, or hospitality setting where appropriate to the culture
- Archive, school, or scribal setting where appropriate
- Local outskirts, farms, camps, or nearby landmarks

These are narrative locations and activity choices. They do not need to be separate explorable maps.

Each destination should have:
- A compact cultural-life profile covering local foods, ingredients, common meals, drinks, hospitality, and feasts.
- A music profile covering instruments, performers, social contexts, and what can or cannot be reconstructed historically.
- A storytelling profile covering myths, legends, epics, fables, proverbs, and the people who tell them.
- A representative cast drawn from ordinary households, crafts, trade, performance, religion, politics, and military life.
- A distinctive visual identity.
- A short atmospheric introduction.
- Local characters and recurring visitors.
- Local goods and commercial opportunities.
- Regional language and cultural context.
- Historical status at the current date.
- Available routes and travel requirements.
- Events that can occur there.
- Changes based on the historical timeline and player choices.

## 5. The protagonist

### 5.1 The player's role and starting identity

The protagonist begins as a traveling merchant: someone who knows how to recognize value, negotiate, arrange passage, read people, and make a living by moving between communities. They are not initially a famous hero, ruler, priest, or warrior. Their public identity is modest enough that they can enter ordinary spaces and speak with people from many walks of life.

The player should be able to shape the protagonist's outward identity:
- Name or commonly used name.
- Appearance and presentation.
- A small number of background details that influence opening dialogue or contacts.
- Temperament expressed through choices rather than a rigid class.
- The kinds of goods, routes, and relationships they initially know.

The deeper origin should remain partially obscured. The game should not begin with a lengthy prophecy explaining who the protagonist is. Their mystery is something the player discovers through people, places, names, stories, and apparent coincidences.

### 5.2 A merchant, traveler, and cultural intermediary

The protagonist's occupation is the narrative key that allows them to cross social boundaries. A merchant can have a reason to speak with a farmer about grain, a sailor about a route, a craftsperson about materials, a priest about an offering, a noble about a contract, or a ruler about the movement of goods.

They are not simply a shopkeeper. They are:
- A traveler who sees how different communities live.
- A negotiator who learns what people value.
- A carrier of goods, letters, stories, news, and sometimes people.
- A witness to political and cultural change.
- A person who can connect individuals who would otherwise never meet.
- A recurring presence whose identity becomes stranger as the years pass.

The merchant role should remain meaningful throughout the game. Even when the protagonist is welcomed at a royal court or recognized by a religious figure, they retain the habits and perspective of someone who has spent a lifetime on the road.

### 5.3 Supernatural roots and the merchant-traveler archetype

The protagonist has supernatural roots. The possibility that they are a demigod—or a divine being living under a mortal identity—should become increasingly compelling without being conclusively explained.

Their nature is associated with travel, exchange, roads, boundaries, language, messages, hospitality, luck, negotiation, and the movement of people and goods. These associations make them recognizable in different cultural settings, but the game must not assume that every culture has an identical merchant god or that distinct deities are interchangeable.

Different civilizations may have their own divine figures associated with commerce, travelers, messengers, roads, cunning, luck, boundaries, protection, or hospitality. These figures can provide a rich comparative framework for the protagonist's mystery. The protagonist may be interpreted as:
- A local god traveling in disguise.
- A demigod or child of a deity.
- A human who has been blessed, cursed, or adopted by a divine power.
- A recurring figure from an older story, recognized under a different name.
- A stranger onto whom people project the traits of their own gods and heroes.
- Something for which no local tradition has an adequate explanation.

The game should explore similarities without collapsing differences. A Greek or Mycenaean figure associated with trade and cunning is not automatically the same being as a Mesopotamian messenger, an Egyptian divine intermediary, a South Asian divine traveler, or a Chinese culture hero. Their domains, stories, rituals, and historical development differ. The protagonist may echo one or more of these figures, but the relationship should be a mystery the narrative explores—not a universal equivalence declared by the codex.

### 5.4 Other names, titles, and identities

The protagonist may be known by different names in different regions and periods. These names are a central narrative device.

They may appear as:
- A local epithet meaning traveler, messenger, guide, stranger, guest, trader, or boundary-crosser.
- A name used in a folktale or local religious tradition.
- A title bestowed by a ruler, temple, or community.
- A name that appears in a story older than the protagonist's apparent life.
- A name spoken by a character who insists they have met the protagonist before.
- A name that resembles a divine or heroic figure but is not an exact match.

The names should be culturally and linguistically researched. They must not be chosen merely because they sound exotic or because a modern audience recognizes a famous god. If the protagonist is associated with an actual deity, the game should establish whether this is a direct identity, an epithet, a local interpretation, a mistaken identification, or an unresolved possibility.

Some names may be contradictory. One culture may regard the figure as benevolent; another may tell stories of a trickster who steals, deceives, or tests travelers. A name might be revered in one place and feared in another.

### 5.5 Longevity and the passage of generations

The protagonist's supernatural nature permits them to remain present across an extended chronology. The exact mechanism should remain uncertain, but the narrative must be consistent.

Preferred direction:
- The protagonist ages very slowly, stops visibly aging after a point, or changes in a way that is never fully explained.
- They remain vulnerable to injury, imprisonment, loss, deprivation, and emotional suffering.
- Their apparent age may be noticed by a small number of recurring characters.
- Most people do not recognize them across decades unless they have a reason to do so.
- Some characters may believe the protagonist is the same person as a figure in an inherited story.
- Descendants may recognize a name, token, portrait, or family account even when they have never met the protagonist.

Longevity should create emotional stakes rather than erase them. The protagonist can outlive friends, lovers, rivals, patrons, and entire political orders. They may have to decide whether to reveal the truth, leave before people notice, or allow someone to know.

### 5.6 The protagonist's relationship with actual gods

Actual gods may appear in the story, but their presence should be handled with deliberate ambiguity and cultural specificity.

Possible forms:
- A deity appears in human guise and interacts with the protagonist.
- A figure in a story or ritual seems to describe the protagonist.
- A stranger demonstrates knowledge that should be impossible.
- A religious specialist claims to recognize a divine presence.
- A character is later revealed—or strongly implied—to have been more than human.
- A god appears in a dream, omen, procession, or symbolic encounter rather than as a conventional NPC.

Not every supernatural encounter needs to be a deity. Some should remain human, mistaken, theatrical, manipulative, or coincidental. The player should not be able to identify divine beings through a simple visual marker.

Where a deity is directly represented, the writing should draw from the relevant culture's own traditions and period evidence. Avoid treating gods as a single shared cast who travel between civilizations under different names unless that syncretism is historically appropriate to the specific time and place.

### 5.7 Player agency and characterization

The player shapes the protagonist through decisions, not a rigid alignment meter.

Possible tendencies include:
- Compassionate or pragmatic.
- Cautious or daring.
- Diplomatic or confrontational.
- Curious or guarded.
- Generous or transactional.
- Devout, skeptical, or uncertain.
- Loyal to particular people or willing to move on.
- Interested in preserving knowledge or focused on immediate survival.

The protagonist should have a recognizable voice and worldview, but dialogue choices should allow meaningful expression. Avoid making every response a simplistic good, neutral, or evil option.

The player should also shape how the protagonist understands their own nature. They may embrace a divine interpretation, reject it, investigate it, or remain undecided. These choices should alter relationships and ending possibilities without revealing a definitive cosmic answer.

### 5.8 The protagonist's personal arc

The protagonist needs an emotional arc as substantial as the historical story.

The arc should explore:
- What it means to belong when one can never remain unchanged with a place.
- Whether long life is a gift, burden, punishment, or obligation.
- Whether the protagonist's role is to observe, intervene, protect, profit, or connect.
- How they respond to being worshiped, feared, misidentified, or used.
- Whether their divine associations are something they chose or something imposed on them.
- What they owe to people who trust them.
- Whether memory is enough to preserve a person or a culture.
- Whether a traveler can ever truly have a home.

The central emotional question is not simply “What is the protagonist?” It is “What will this person do with the extraordinary amount of time and influence they have been given?”

## 6. Main narrative structure

### 6.1 Narrative format

The game is a long, chaptered journey with a central narrative and substantial regional storylines.

The player has freedom to choose destinations and pursue relationships, but the historical timeline continues. The main narrative is not a linear sequence of mandatory missions. It is a web of events, people, journeys, and discoveries that converges on a set of possible endings.

The game should be extensive enough to make the protagonist's long existence and the breadth of the world feel earned. The story should not rush from the opening to the collapse and then abruptly end.

### 6.2 Proposed macrostructure

The following acts are a narrative framework, not a fixed calendar. Exact dates and events should be researched and finalized during historical design.

#### Prologue — The Merchant

- Introduce the protagonist in or near Ugarit around 1230 BCE.
- Establish the basic trading loop.
- Introduce the first small cast: a merchant contact, a ship captain, a local household, and a temple or ritual figure.
- Teach buying, selling, inventory, travel preparation, dialogue, and the journal.
- Present the first unusual encounter: a name, dream, symbol, or recognition that the protagonist cannot explain.
- Establish that the player is a traveler, not a chosen ruler or military hero.

#### Act I — The Connected World

- The player explores the eastern Mediterranean and nearby regions.
- Trade networks are functioning, although local rivalries and tensions are visible.
- The player meets recurring characters and begins building a reputation.
- The player discovers that language and relationships can open doors.
- Early political relationships introduce factional influence.
- The player begins to hear about distant routes east and south.
- The protagonist's unusual names and apparent history become a recurring mystery.

#### Act II — Rumors and Fractures

- Reports of raids, shortages, political tensions, disrupted trade, and displaced people become more frequent.
- Some routes become riskier; others become more valuable.
- The player encounters people who have lost access to their homes or livelihoods.
- The player can help individuals and groups relocate, find work, reconnect with relatives, or secure protection.
- Factions become more consequential: a relationship with one power may help in one region but create suspicion elsewhere.
- The protagonist's apparent longevity and unfamiliar names become harder to explain.

#### Act III — The Breaking of the Old Networks

- Major historical disruptions affect different regions at different times.
- Familiar destinations change. Some are destroyed, abandoned, weakened, occupied, or transformed.
- Some characters die, disappear, flee, or become separated from their families.
- Trade becomes less predictable and some old routes cease to be viable.
- The player must decide where to go and whom to help.
- The story emphasizes human consequences rather than a global collapse simulation.
- The player may preserve records, rescue people, carry knowledge, or help communities establish new lives.

#### Act IV — New Roads

- New political and commercial arrangements emerge.
- The player follows refugee and merchant networks into regions that were previously peripheral to the main story.
- Some people encountered during the crisis reappear in new communities.
- New generations inherit stories, obligations, and relationships.
- The player can travel farther south and east, including into South Asia and China.
- The protagonist's different names become a more explicit narrative thread.
- The player discovers that the meaning of their identity changes across cultures and time.

#### Act V — The Long Memory

- The protagonist's extraordinary longevity is no longer easy to dismiss.
- The player encounters descendants, successors, and people who have inherited stories about them.
- Old choices return in unexpected forms.
- The player may revisit places that have changed beyond recognition.
- The central mystery approaches a meaningful resolution, but need not become fully explained.
- The player's accumulated relationships, regional journeys, faction standing, language abilities, and decisions determine which final storylines become available.

#### Endgame — The Roads That Remain

The ending is determined by the player's journey, not a single final boss or one mandatory destination.

Potential ending families include:
- **The Keeper of Names:** The protagonist becomes a living custodian of stories, records, and memories across cultures. One that survives Dark Ages.
- **The Friend of Peoples:** The protagonist's most consequential legacy is the people and communities they helped survive and rebuild. His legacy will live on in the pantheons of the civilizations these people create.
- **The King's Companion:** The protagonist becomes closely tied to one or more ruling houses, courts, or political legacies and elevates a lineage into god status through their accomplishments and impact.
- **The Merchant Beyond the Horizon:** The protagonist continues along the far eastern or southern routes, choosing discovery and movement over settling in one place.
- **The One Who Stayed:** The protagonist establishes a lasting home and community, accepting a more rooted life.
- **The Unremembered Traveler:** The protagonist's actions matter deeply to individuals but leave little public trace.
- **The Divine Name:** The protagonist accepts, rejects, or continues to evade the possibility of their divine identity.
- **The Unfinished Road:** The protagonist's story remains open, with the player able to continue traveling after the main ending.

These are thematic ending families, not mutually exclusive achievements. The final ending may combine elements depending on the player's relationships and decisions.
Regardless of the ending, we want to emphasize impact and weave an emotional throughline firmly.

### 6.3 Narrative pacing

The game should balance:
- Main historical developments.
- Regional story arcs.
- Recurring character arcs.
- Merchant and travel activities.
- Optional discoveries and mysteries.
- Quiet everyday scenes.

Do not make every journey trigger a major crisis. Some journeys should be peaceful. Some destinations should offer several hours of ordinary life and relationship-building before history intrudes.

The player should be allowed to spend time in a place, learn its language, build relationships, and become attached to it.

## 7. Recurring characters and character arcs

### 7.1 Core principle

Characters are not disposable encounter content. Important characters have their own lives, relationships, work, beliefs, ambitions, limitations, and arcs. They should feel like inhabitants of a particular society rather than modern quest-givers wearing ancient clothing.

The player should meet people in the context of their ordinary lives: buying grain, repairing a boat, preparing a festival, tending animals, composing a song, settling a dispute, carrying water, making a delivery, training soldiers, caring for children, or negotiating a marriage. Their stories can intersect with politics and historical upheaval, but they should not all be defined by the player.

### 7.2 Character professions and social worlds

The cast should represent a broad cross-section of ancient society. The exact roles and status of each profession must be researched for the region and date.

**Everyday households**
- Farmers, herders, fishers, gardeners, millers, cooks, brewers, bakers, and household managers.
- Parents, children, elders, siblings, spouses, apprentices, and extended families.
- People who maintain homes, prepare food, care for animals, make clothing, and manage seasonal work.

**Craftspeople and makers**
- Potters, weavers, dyers, carpenters, shipwrights, metalworkers, stoneworkers, leatherworkers, jewelers, and basket makers.
- Apprentices and workshop owners.
- Specialists whose knowledge is valuable to merchants, rulers, temples, and communities.

**Merchants and travelers**
- Local traders, long-distance merchants, brokers, ship owners, sailors, caravan leaders, porters, guides, interpreters, and messengers.
- People who know particular routes, languages, markets, and political conditions.
- Commercial rivals who may become allies, friends, or recurring antagonists.

**Performers and keepers of oral tradition**
- Bards, singers, musicians, dancers, storytellers, praise-singers, lament singers, and performers at feasts or ceremonies.
- People who preserve genealogies, heroic tales, local legends, and accounts of migration.
- Traveling performers who carry stories between communities and adapt them for new audiences.

The term “bard” should be used carefully where it is a convenient design label rather than a historically attested local occupational title. The game should use culturally and linguistically appropriate names for performers in each setting.

**Religious and ritual life**
- Priests, priestesses, temple administrators, ritual specialists, diviners, healers, ascetics where appropriate, funerary specialists, and custodians of local shrines.
- People responsible for offerings, festivals, purification, burial, divination, and the maintenance of sacred spaces.
- Individuals whose religious roles are also embedded in families, politics, or local economies.

**Political and military life**
- Kings, queens, princes, nobles, local rulers, governors, envoys, scribes, administrators, judges, guards, soldiers, commanders, and veterans.
- People responsible for fortifications, patrols, diplomacy, taxation, and the movement of troops.
- Court attendants and household officials who may wield influence despite not holding the highest formal rank.

**Knowledge and specialist roles**
- Scribes, record keepers, teachers, healers, physicians, astronomers, diviners, surveyors, and people with specialized technical knowledge.
- Individuals who know how to read particular scripts, interpret omens, calculate measures, or preserve records.
- Scholars and experts should be represented according to the institutions and forms of knowledge actually present in each culture.

The cast should include people of different wealth, status, age, gender, origin, and access to power. Avoid presenting ancient societies as socially uniform.
That said, we do want certain recurring characters among those who are capable of traveling from city to city. Their reasons for travel may vary, but a common cast among the wider net of individuals the player can interact with can act as an anchor.

### 7.3 Historical figures

Actual historical figures may appear as characters when the evidence and narrative justify it.

They may include:
- Rulers and members of royal families.
- Military leaders and diplomats.
- Religious or political authorities.
- Known scribes, scholars, or other identifiable individuals.
- Figures whose existence is attested indirectly through inscriptions, archives, or later traditions.

Historical figures should not be inserted merely as recognizable cameos. Their portrayal must respect chronology, known relationships, political context, and the limits of surviving evidence.

For each historical figure:
- Record the evidence for their existence and dates.
- Separate documented actions from reconstructed motivations.
- Avoid inventing private conversations and presenting them as fact.
- Use fictional dialogue only as clearly authored dramatic reconstruction.
- Do not force the protagonist into the center of events that are not plausibly connected to them.
- Allow the historical figure to have interests and agency independent of the player.

The player may meet a ruler, negotiate with an official, or witness a public event. They should not necessarily become the secret cause of every major historical development.

### 7.4 Gods, divine disguises, and mythic figures

The game may include actual gods, demigods, culture heroes, spirits, and legendary figures as characters or apparent characters.

This is a major source of narrative possibility, but it requires a disciplined approach.

A divine figure may:
- Appear openly in a culturally appropriate sacred or mythic context.
- Travel in human guise.
- Be mistaken for an ordinary traveler, merchant, performer, soldier, or stranger.
- Speak through a dream, omen, ritual, animal, or symbolic event.
- Be known by different titles or aspects in different local traditions.
- Intervene in a character's life for reasons unrelated to the protagonist.
- Recognize the protagonist—or pretend to recognize them.

The game should not turn every unusual stranger into a god. Many encounters should remain entirely human. When a character is divine, the story should draw from the relevant mythology and avoid flattening distinct traditions into a generic fantasy cosmology.

The protagonist's merchant-traveler nature offers a recurring thematic counterpart: many cultures have divine or semi-divine figures associated with roads, exchange, messages, cunning, luck, hospitality, boundaries, or the movement between worlds. The game can explore these resonances while preserving each figure's distinctive identity.

### 7.5 Mythology, folklore, and oral storytelling

Stories should be part of daily life, not confined to codex entries.

Characters may tell stories:
- At meals and feasts.
- During long journeys.
- While working in workshops or fields.
- At festivals and religious gatherings.
- In taverns, inns, harbors, camps, and household courtyards.
- To entertain children.
- To explain a local custom, landscape, family, or sacred place.
- To praise a ruler or criticize an enemy.
- To preserve the memory of a destroyed home.

The game should draw on the myths, epics, fables, proverbs, folktales, heroic cycles, and ritual narratives that are appropriate to each culture and period.

Possible story forms include:
- Creation and origin stories.
- Divine conflicts and journeys.
- Heroic exploits and tragic returns.
- Trickster tales and clever bargains.
- Animal fables.
- Flood, destruction, and renewal traditions.
- Tales explaining stars, rivers, mountains, plants, and customs.
- Genealogies and stories of founding ancestors.
- Laments for the dead or for lost cities.
- Local ghost stories and warnings for travelers.

The player may hear different versions of a story in different places. Stories change as they travel. A tale may be adapted, misunderstood, translated, politicized, or used to make a point about current events.

The game must distinguish a story's cultural role from whether its events are treated as literal historical fact. A myth is not simply a false account; it can express values, social memory, religious understanding, political legitimacy, and a community's sense of the world.

### 7.6 Music and performers as characters

Musicians and storytellers should have their own arcs and social roles.

They may:
- Perform at feasts, courts, temples, markets, funerals, and public celebrations.
- Travel between settlements.
- Carry news and stories.
- Be patronized by a ruler or wealthy household.
- Preserve a local song or lament.
- Adapt a story for a different audience.
- Become displaced and seek a new patron.
- Recognize a melody, name, or phrase associated with the protagonist.

A recurring performer can provide a particularly effective way to show historical change: a song heard in one city may later be performed with altered words by refugees in another region.

### 7.7 Character arc structure

A significant character should have:
- An introduction rooted in their daily life.
- A personal situation, motivation, and immediate concern.
- Relationships with other characters.
- A set of possible developments.
- A plausible location and travel history.
- Reactions to relevant historical events.
- A possible resolution, disappearance, or death.
- Optional reappearances and callbacks.
- A distinct voice, worldview, and way of speaking.

Character arcs may span several chapters or decades. Some characters may recur over a short period; others may be represented through descendants, apprentices, inherited stories, or family obligations.

### 7.8 Family and generational continuity

Families make the passage of time tangible.

The player may:
- Help a family escape a threatened settlement.
- Reunite relatives separated during flight.
- Find a place for a family to settle.
- Help a craftsperson establish a workshop.
- Support a child or apprentice who later becomes an adult character.
- Encounter descendants who know stories about the protagonist.
- Discover that a decision made decades earlier shaped a family's fortunes.

The game should not require the player to track every NPC's family tree. Generational continuity should be authored for important families and selected communities.

### 7.9 Character reappearance rules

Reappearances should feel earned and plausible:
- A merchant travels along a known route.
- A refugee relocates to a destination the player can later visit.
- A performer joins a caravan or ship.
- A political appointment moves a character to a court.
- A family member carries news or an object.
- A character's descendants inherit a relationship, debt, or story.

Do not teleport characters simply to provide fan service. If a character reappears unexpectedly, the story should explain how they arrived there.

### 7.10 Character consequences

The player's actions may influence:
- Trust and affection.
- Commercial cooperation.
- Access to places and people.
- Willingness to share information.
- Whether someone travels with or assists the player.
- Whether a character's family survives or remains together.
- Whether a character becomes a rival, ally, patron, or enemy.
- Whether a story reaches a particular resolution.

Not every outcome should be visible immediately. Delayed consequences are important to the feeling that characters have lives beyond the player's immediate attention.

### 7.11 Character writing standard

Every major character should be designed around more than occupation and quest function.

A character brief should include:
- Name, pronunciation, and culturally appropriate form of address.
- Approximate age and life stage.
- Home region, language(s), and social context.
- Occupation and daily routine.
- Household, family, and important relationships.
- Beliefs, values, fears, ambitions, and contradictions.
- What they know and what they misunderstand.
- Their relationship to local food, music, stories, and rituals.
- Their attitude toward the protagonist and the protagonist's unusual names.
- Their arc across the timeline.
- Their possible fate and reappearance conditions.

The goal is not to write an encyclopedia for every passerby. A small number of specific details—a favorite food, a song they know, a proverb they repeat, a person they miss, a craft they take pride in—can make a character feel alive.

## 8. Refugees, migration, and rebuilding

### 8.1 Narrative importance

Refugee movement should be one of the central human threads of the game.

The collapse and transformation of political networks should be felt through individuals and groups moving across the map. People do not simply vanish when a settlement falls. They seek relatives, employment, safety, land, patronage, and new opportunities.

The player can become an important intermediary in these movements.

### 8.2 Player actions

The player may:
- Escort people to a safer destination.
- Pay for passage aboard a ship or with a caravan.
- Negotiate safe passage with a faction.
- Find a patron, employer, or host family.
- Help reunite separated relatives.
- Carry letters, tokens, records, or personal belongings.
- Help a craftsperson find a workshop.
- Help a merchant re-establish a business.
- Secure temporary food, lodging, or supplies.
- Choose whether to prioritize one group over another when resources are limited.
- Return later to discover what became of people they helped.

These actions should be narrative decisions with modest, comprehensible resource costs. The game should not turn refugee resettlement into a population-management minigame.

### 8.3 Migration chains

A group helped in one region may:
1. Reach a temporary refuge.
2. Encounter difficulties establishing themselves.
3. Find work or a patron.
4. Split, reunite, or move again.
5. Become part of a new community.
6. Reappear later through a character, descendant, letter, or story.

Not every chain needs all stages. Some outcomes should remain uncertain.

### 8.4 Historical care

Do not imply that every migration in the game was caused by the Late Bronze Age collapse. Different groups move for different reasons, and the causes of specific historical migrations are often uncertain.

Fictional refugee stories should be plausible and grounded in the period's material and social conditions. Avoid assigning modern national identities to ancient populations.

## 9. Trading and merchant gameplay

### 9.1 Role of trading

Trading is a meaningful but approachable gameplay system. It provides:
- A reason to travel.
- A source of money and progression.
- Opportunities to meet people.
- A way to establish commercial relationships.
- Practical choices about cargo, risk, and destination.
- A way to participate in the ancient world's interconnectedness.

It is not intended to become a detailed economic simulation.

### 9.2 Core loop

1. Arrive at a destination.
2. Learn what is happening locally.
3. Talk to characters and discover opportunities.
4. Buy and sell goods.
5. Accept contracts, favors, or commercial arrangements.
6. Prepare inventory and supplies.
7. Choose a route and travel mode.
8. Experience travel events.
9. Arrive and discover the consequences of prior choices.

### 9.3 Goods

Goods should be regionally and historically appropriate.

Potential categories:
- Food and agricultural products.
- Textiles and finished clothing.
- Metals and metal goods.
- Timber and woodworking materials.
- Ceramics and household goods.
- Stone, beads, and luxury materials.
- Incense, resins, oils, and aromatics.
- Livestock and animal products.
- Tools and crafted goods.
- Writing materials, records, and specialist items.

Examples may include copper, tin, bronze objects, silver, gold, grain, wine, olive oil, textiles, timber, lapis lazuli, carnelian, incense, ivory, ebony, and pottery. Availability must be researched by region and date.

Do not assume every item is freely traded everywhere. Some goods may be controlled by rulers, temples, merchant houses, or particular communities.

### 9.4 Inventory and capacity

The player has a finite carrying capacity, determined by their current transport and arrangements.

Inventory decisions should be simple:
- Cargo has weight or space.
- Some goods are fragile, perishable, or risky to carry.
- Travel mode affects capacity and cost.
- Special items may occupy a separate protected slot.
- The interface should make it easy to compare value, weight, and likely usefulness.

Avoid complex packing physics or individual item condition simulation.

### 9.5 Prices and markets

Prices should be authored or generated through simple location- and event-based rules.

Factors may include:
- Regional availability.
- Local demand.
- Historical disruption.
- Season or harvest.
- Political restrictions.
- Faction relationships.
- Current local events.
- Special contracts.

The player should be able to learn where goods are valued without requiring spreadsheets or constant price optimization.

Prices should be believable but not claim false historical precision. The economy is a game abstraction.

### 9.6 Contracts and commercial relationships

Examples:
- Deliver goods to a known merchant.
- Find a specific material.
- Carry a letter or sealed package.
- Secure a supply for a household, workshop, or temple.
- Help a merchant recover a lost shipment.
- Arrange a purchase for a court.
- Find a buyer for a refugee craftsperson's work.
- Carry goods through a politically sensitive route.

Contracts should create stories and relationships, not just fetch quests.

### 9.7 Transport and progression

The player may improve their travel capabilities through:
- Better pack animals or carts where appropriate.
- Access to reliable ships or caravans.
- Commercial partnerships.
- Trusted guides.
- Storage arrangements.
- Better equipment and supplies.
- Reputation that reduces costs or opens routes.

Transport should be historically appropriate to the region and period. Do not give the player a universal vehicle that trivializes geography.

### 9.8 Wealth and its limits

Money should matter, but it should not solve every problem. Some doors open through trust, language, obligation, kinship, ritual knowledge, or political favor rather than payment.

The player should not need to become extraordinarily wealthy to see the main narrative. The game must be completable through a balanced mix of trading, relationships, and story choices.

## 10. Travel and encounters

### 10.1 Travel is the central activity

Travel should feel like movement through a living world, not a loading screen between shops.

The player chooses:
- Destination.
- Route.
- Travel mode or transport arrangement.
- Whether to travel alone, with a group, or under a patron's protection.
- Whether to take a safer, faster, cheaper, or more uncertain route when those options exist.

The game then presents a sequence of travel events and atmospheric moments.

### 10.2 Travel modes

Possible modes include:
- Walking.
- Donkey or other appropriate pack-animal travel.
- Ox cart or other regionally appropriate wheeled transport.
- Caravan travel.
- River travel.
- Coastal or open-water sailing.
- Travel with an official, military, or merchant party.

Availability must be date- and region-specific. The game should not use anachronistic transport simply because it is familiar to modern players.

### 10.3 Journey structure

A journey can contain:
- A departure scene.
- One or more encounters.
- A choice or complication.
- A quiet travel vignette.
- A destination arrival scene.

Not every trip needs several branching events. Short routes should remain short. Long journeys should offer a stronger sense of distance and discovery.

### 10.4 Encounter categories

- Social: travelers, families, merchants, messengers, guides.
- Commercial: trading opportunities, lost cargo, disputes, contracts.
- Political: patrols, envoys, tolls, military checkpoints, faction representatives.
- Military: raiders, soldiers, besieged settlements, conflict zones.
- Migration: refugees, displaced households, pastoral movements.
- Environmental: storms, floods, drought, difficult terrain, seasonal conditions.
- Religious: processions, shrines, rituals, omens, sacred places.
- Wildlife: regionally and historically appropriate animals.
- Discovery: ruins, unusual objects, inscriptions, unfamiliar communities.
- Personal: recurring characters, letters, rumors, memories, and protagonist mysteries.

### 10.5 Encounter design

Encounters should be curated and contextual. They are selected based on:
- Route and region.
- Date and historical phase.
- Travel mode.
- Season or environmental condition.
- Player relationships and faction standing.
- Known characters and their current locations.
- Previous decisions.
- Relevant language ability.
- Current narrative arc.

Randomness can select among eligible authored encounters, but major events and important character moments must be scheduled or condition-driven.

### 10.6 Conflict resolution

Combat should not be the default solution. Most encounters should support some combination of:
- Conversation.
- Negotiation.
- Payment.
- Deception.
- Appealing to shared contacts or faction relationships.
- Showing a letter, token, or proof of status.
- Avoidance or retreat.
- Helping someone.
- Accepting a cost or risk.

If physical danger is included, keep it lightweight and consistent with the game's narrative focus. The player is a merchant and traveler, not a battlefield commander.

### 10.7 War zones and safe passage

Faction relationships should have direct travel consequences.

For example:
- A favorable relationship with a regional power may allow passage through a checkpoint.
- A letter from a ruler or official may provide protection.
- A merchant guild or caravan leader may arrange safe travel.
- A hostile faction may refuse entry or demand a toll.
- A neutral local guide may help the player avoid a dangerous route.
- A relationship with one side may make the opposing side suspicious.

Safe passage should not be guaranteed by a single high reputation score. Context, current conflict, local authority, language, and the specific character involved should matter.

## 11. Language as a progression mechanic

### 11.1 Design intent

Language should be a benefit and a source of discovery, not a barrier that prevents the player from enjoying the game.

The player should be able to communicate sufficiently to progress through the main story, using gestures, interpreters, shared languages, familiar contacts, or narrative abstraction where necessary.

Learning a language improves the quality and depth of interaction. It unlocks nuance, trust, information, opportunities, and alternate solutions.

### 11.2 Learning languages

Language ability improves through:
- Spending time in a region where the language is spoken.
- Speaking with characters.
- Repeated interactions with the same person.
- Working with a tutor or interpreter.
- Reading inscriptions, labels, letters, or documents where appropriate.
- Completing language-related discoveries.
- Participating in local activities and rituals.

The game should recognize that languages are not always identical to political borders. Some characters may be multilingual, use trade languages, or speak regional variants.

### 11.3 Proficiency levels

A simple progression model is recommended:

1. **Unfamiliar:** The player recognizes little or nothing. Basic communication is possible through context, gestures, or help.
2. **Basic:** The player understands greetings, prices, simple requests, and common expressions.
3. **Conversational:** The player can hold ordinary conversations and understand more of a character's intent.
4. **Fluent:** The player can discuss complex subjects, negotiate, recognize subtle implications, and interact without an interpreter.
5. **Deep familiarity:** The player understands idioms, cultural references, formal speech, literary or ritual language, and subtle social distinctions where the content supports it.

Not every language needs all levels represented in every scene.

### 11.4 Language benefits

Higher proficiency can unlock:
- More dialogue choices.
- Better prices or more transparent negotiations.
- Recognition of deception, sarcasm, or double meanings.
- Access to local stories and rumors.
- Ability to understand inscriptions or documents.
- Greater trust from certain characters.
- Participation in rituals or formal settings.
- Better diplomatic outcomes.
- Alternate ways to resolve travel encounters.
- Recognition of the protagonist's unusual names or titles.

Language should not become a universal key that automatically grants faction favor. Cultural knowledge and personal relationships still matter.

### 11.5 Interpreters and multilingual characters

Interpreters should be recurring characters where possible. They may become companions, trusted contacts, commercial partners, or important political intermediaries.

An interpreter can help the player communicate before they learn a language, but may:
- Charge a fee.
- Have their own loyalties.
- Omit or reinterpret information.
- Become unavailable.
- Provide access to a new region or faction.

The player should not be punished for using interpreters. Learning the language simply creates additional possibilities.

### 11.6 Language presentation

The game should not display every conversation as incomprehensible foreign text. That would make the mechanic tedious.

Instead:
- The interface can communicate the protagonist's approximate understanding.
- Early interactions may show uncertainty or summarize meaning.
- Higher proficiency reveals more exact dialogue.
- Important scenes should remain understandable.
- Short phrases in ancient languages may be used sparingly for atmosphere, with translations available.
- Language learning should be visible through the journal or character interface, but should not dominate the HUD.

## 12. Factions, politics, and reputation

### 12.1 Purpose

The faction system represents the player's relationships with political, commercial, religious, and regional groups.

It should influence access, trade, travel, character interactions, and story outcomes. It should not become a grand strategy diplomacy simulator.

### 12.2 Faction types

- Kingdoms, states, and royal courts.
- Regional rulers and local authorities.
- Merchant houses and commercial networks.
- Temples and religious institutions.
- Military organizations or garrisons.
- Guild-like craft or trade communities where historically appropriate.
- Caravan groups and maritime networks.
- Local communities and extended families.

Not every faction needs a visible numerical reputation bar.

### 12.3 Relationship dimensions

A faction relationship may reflect:
- Trust.
- Favor.
- Obligation.
- Commercial standing.
- Political alignment.
- Religious or cultural respect.
- Fear or suspicion.
- Outstanding debts or favors.

The system should use a small number of understandable underlying values and present them through narrative feedback.

### 12.4 How reputation changes

Reputation can change through:
- Completing contracts.
- Helping or harming members.
- Supporting one faction during a dispute.
- Respecting local customs.
- Speaking the language.
- Providing valuable information.
- Carrying official letters or gifts.
- Breaking promises.
- Trading fairly or dishonestly.
- Helping refugees or communities associated with a faction.
- Publicly taking sides in a conflict.

### 12.5 Political access

Relationships may unlock:
- Audience with a ruler or official.
- Safe passage.
- Reduced tolls.
- Protected lodging.
- Access to markets or restricted goods.
- Invitations to ceremonies.
- Information about political developments.
- Introductions to important characters.
- Diplomatic or commercial contracts.

The player may become a trusted associate of a king, noble, ruler, or court, but should remain a traveler with their own agenda.

### 12.6 Conflicting loyalties

Relationships should create meaningful tension.

Helping one faction may:
- Damage relations with a rival.
- Put a merchant contact at risk.
- Close a route.
- Create suspicion in another region.
- Force the player to choose between a personal promise and political safety.

The player should not be required to join one permanent faction. They can maintain relationships across borders, though doing so may become difficult during conflict.

### 12.7 Local and personal reputation

Faction standing is not the same as personal relationships.

A ruler may trust the player while a local official dislikes them. A merchant house may welcome the player even though its political patron is hostile. A family may care little about the player's reputation at court.

The game must keep these distinctions.

## 13. Religion, mythology, ritual, and the supernatural

### 13.1 Cultural specificity

Religious life should be regionally and historically specific. The game should not merge all ancient religions into one universal pantheon or treat every deity as a different name for the same being.

Each region should have its own traditions, sacred spaces, ritual specialists, stories, festivals, funerary customs, and understandings of divine power. These traditions may overlap, influence one another, or be interpreted through contact, but the game should show how and why that happens.

### 13.2 Gods as characters

Actual gods may appear as characters, including in human disguise. Some may be central to a regional storyline; others may appear briefly, indirectly, or ambiguously.

Possible presentations:
- A human stranger whose words or actions echo a known divine story.
- A traveler who appears at a threshold, crossroads, harbor, or border.
- A figure who visits a ruler, household, or religious specialist in disguise.
- A deity who is openly recognized during a ritual or mythic event.
- A divine presence encountered in a dream or vision.
- A character whose identity is never confirmed but whose actions are difficult to explain.

A divine figure should have motives and a cultural identity, not merely dispense quests or supernatural rewards. Gods may be generous, capricious, jealous, playful, frightening, protective, or indifferent, depending on the source tradition and the needs of the story.

### 13.3 Comparative divine archetypes without false equivalence

The protagonist is associated with travel, trade, messages, cunning, luck, boundaries, and exchange. Different cultures have their own figures connected to some of these domains.

The writing team should research those figures individually. They may serve as:
- Mirrors of the protagonist.
- Possible relatives, patrons, rivals, or counterparts.
- Figures who recognize the protagonist under a local name.
- Sources of contradictory stories about the protagonist.
- Evidence that similar human concerns produce different divine traditions.

Do not assume every civilization has one direct equivalent of a merchant-traveler god. Some traditions may distribute these roles among several deities, spirits, heroes, or human culture figures. Some may not have a close equivalent at all. Those differences are valuable worldbuilding, not gaps to be filled artificially.

### 13.4 Myth, legend, and oral tradition

Myths and stories should appear throughout ordinary gameplay.

The player may hear:
- Epic and heroic narratives.
- Stories of gods and divine journeys.
- Fables and trickster tales.
- Founding legends and genealogies.
- Local explanations for landscapes and customs.
- Stories about dangerous roads, rivers, mountains, and seas.
- Laments, funeral songs, and remembrance traditions.
- Stories told to children or travelers.

The same story may have multiple versions. A performer, priest, farmer, soldier, and ruler may tell it differently because they have different purposes and perspectives.

The player may learn that a story is associated with a particular temple, city, family, or political authority. Stories can influence how people interpret events and the protagonist's identity.

### 13.5 Ritual and religious interaction

The player may:
- Visit temples and shrines.
- Attend public festivals or ceremonies.
- Speak with religious specialists.
- Make offerings where appropriate.
- Learn local ritual customs.
- Carry sacred or symbolic objects.
- Seek interpretations of dreams and omens.
- Become known to a religious institution.
- Commission or support a ritual, where socially and historically appropriate.

Religious interactions may affect relationships and access, but there should be no universal god-favor meter.

### 13.6 The protagonist's mystery

The protagonist's possible divinity is expressed through ambiguity:
- Other names.
- Unusual longevity.
- Dreams.
- Recognition across generations.
- Recurring symbols.
- Contradictory stories.
- Different interpretations by different religious traditions.
- Encounters with apparent gods in human guise.
- Events that could be coincidence, manipulation, memory, or something supernatural.

The game should not confirm a universal explanation. The player can decide what they believe, but no dialogue option should settle the entire mystery.

## 14. Journal, codex, and memory

The journal is a major feature, not an afterthought.

It should help the player remember a large cast, long storylines, distant locations, and events spread across years.

### 14.1 Journal

The journal records:
- Important events.
- Personal promises and obligations.
- Character encounters.
- Letters and messages.
- Discoveries.
- Major choices and their consequences.
- Unresolved mysteries.
- Travel milestones.
- Historical developments witnessed by the player.

Entries should be written in the protagonist's voice where appropriate. Some entries may be incomplete, uncertain, or biased.

### 14.2 Character index

The player can review:
- Characters encountered.
- Where they were last seen.
- Known relationships.
- Important shared events.
- Current status if known.
- Rumors or uncertain reports.

The index should not reveal information the protagonist has not learned.

### 14.3 Map and route knowledge

The map should distinguish:
- Known destinations.
- Known routes.
- Rumored destinations.
- Routes requiring a guide or contact.
- Routes currently unsafe or unavailable.
- Destinations whose political or physical status has changed.

### 14.4 Historical codex

The codex provides optional context about:
- Regions.
- Peoples and political entities.
- Goods and crafts.
- Languages.
- Religious traditions.
- Historical events.
- Transport and travel.
- Important historical figures.

It should not interrupt the story with lectures. Short entries and optional deeper reading are preferable.

## 15. Art, food, music, stories, and presentation

### 15.1 Visual direction

The game should have a distinctive illustrated presentation that communicates the ancient world through:
- Location paintings or carefully composed illustrated screens.
- Character portraits.
- Maps.
- Event illustrations.
- Small environmental animations.
- UI motifs inspired by period materials and visual traditions.

The art should feel evocative rather than generic fantasy. Avoid making every region sandy, brown, and ruined. The ancient world should be colorful, varied, populated, and materially rich.

### 15.2 Regional identity

Each region should have a distinct visual language based on research:
- Architecture and construction materials.
- Clothing, hairstyles, and adornment.
- Tools, vessels, furniture, and domestic objects.
- Landscapes, vegetation, animals, and climate.
- Patterns, pigments, and decorative traditions.
- Ships, carts, pack animals, and other transport.

Do not use a single generic “ancient civilization” art style for all destinations.

### 15.3 Food and everyday eating

Food should be a recurring part of the world's identity. The player should encounter local ingredients, meals, drinks, food preparation, hospitality customs, market stalls, feasts, and differences between everyday and elite diets.

Each major destination should have a researched **food profile** containing:
- Common staple crops and ingredients.
- Typical household foods and methods of preparation.
- Breads, porridges, stews, pulses, vegetables, fruits, nuts, and animal products where attested or plausible.
- Local fish, game, livestock, and preserved foods where appropriate.
- Oils, fats, spices, herbs, sweeteners, and condiments.
- Fermented drinks and other beverages.
- Food preservation and storage methods.
- Seasonal availability and harvest patterns.
- Differences between ordinary households, travelers, soldiers, temples, and elite feasts.
- Food-related customs, hospitality practices, taboos, and ritual uses where supported.

Food should appear naturally:
- A host offers the player a meal.
- A merchant explains why a particular ingredient is expensive.
- A family prepares food for a festival.
- A caravan negotiates provisions before departure.
- A refugee shares the last of their supplies.
- A court banquet reveals hierarchy and political relationships.
- A character remembers a dish from a lost home.

Food may be a light flavor and relationship system. Accepting hospitality, recognizing a dish, or understanding a custom may create dialogue opportunities. Do not turn the game into a cooking simulator or require the player to manage nutrition.

The design must not invent a famous “national dish” for a civilization when the evidence only supports a range of ingredients and cooking practices. Modern regional cuisines should not be projected unchanged into the Bronze Age.

### 15.4 Music and sound

Music is central to the atmosphere and should be researched as a cultural and social practice, not simply assigned as a generic regional soundtrack.

For each major culture or destination, develop a music profile that considers:
- Instruments supported by archaeological, iconographic, or textual evidence.
- Singing, chanting, instrumental performance, and ensemble practices where known.
- Music's role in feasts, temples, processions, work, mourning, military settings, and entertainment.
- Who performed: professional musicians, court performers, ritual specialists, household members, or traveling entertainers.
- Whether music was associated with particular social groups, ceremonies, or institutions.
- What is known, inferred, or impossible to reconstruct.

The game may use historically informed original compositions. It should not claim to reproduce exact ancient melodies unless a surviving musical source genuinely supports that claim. Where the sound is speculative, the production notes should say so.

Music should be heard diegetically as well as in the score:
- A singer performing in a courtyard.
- A musician entertaining a merchant household.
- A ritual chant or ceremonial performance.
- Work songs or rhythmic accompaniment.
- A lament at a funeral.
- A traveling performer repeating a melody in another region.
- A familiar tune whose words have changed after a community's displacement.

A recurring musical theme can become a narrative motif. A melody heard in one city may later return in another, played by someone who learned it from refugees. This can communicate cultural continuity without requiring exposition.

### 15.5 Stories, legends, and oral culture

Stories are a major way the player learns how people understand their world.

Every major region should have a researched **storytelling profile** containing:
- Important myths, epics, fables, and heroic cycles attested in or relevant to the period.
- Stories about gods, ancestors, rulers, animals, landscapes, origins, and the afterlife.
- Local proverbs, riddles, warnings, and sayings where evidence permits.
- Oral performance contexts and likely audiences.
- The relationship between stories, ritual, political legitimacy, and community identity.
- What is known from contemporary sources versus later written versions.

The player may hear stories from:
- Bards, singers, and traveling performers.
- Priests, priestesses, and ritual specialists.
- Farmers and herders.
- Sailors and caravan travelers.
- Soldiers and veterans.
- Parents and grandparents.
- Rulers and court poets.
- Children repeating local tales.

Stories should be adapted to the speaker. A soldier may tell a boastful version of a battle story; a widow may tell a lament; a priest may emphasize divine order; a farmer may use an animal fable to criticize a local official.

The player can hear variant versions of the same story across languages and regions. Translation may change names, metaphors, or emphasis. Some stories may echo the protagonist's mystery, but the game should avoid making every myth secretly about the protagonist.

### 15.6 Regional cultural profiles

Every major destination should have a compact cultural profile used by writers, artists, designers, and audio teams.

At minimum, it should include:

| Profile area | Required content |
|---|---|
| Food | Staples, common meals, drinks, ingredients, markets, hospitality, feasts |
| Music | Instruments, performance contexts, performers, sound palette, confidence of reconstruction |
| Stories | Myths, legends, epics, fables, proverbs, oral traditions, variant versions |
| Religion | Deities, local cults, rituals, sacred places, funerary practices, religious roles |
| Daily life | Household routines, work, clothing, crafts, social spaces, seasonal rhythms |
| People | Social roles, professions, family structures, local elites, travelers |
| Language | Languages, scripts, multilingualism, forms of address, trade communication |
| Material culture | Architecture, tools, vessels, textiles, ornaments, transport |
| Sensory environment | Weather, landscape, smells, sounds, colors, animals, crowd life |
| Historical context | Political situation, regional changes, trade links, uncertainty and sources |

A destination should not feel complete merely because it has a market, a palace, and a temple. It should feel inhabited. The player should be able to understand what people eat, what they hear, what they make, what they tell children, what they fear, and what they celebrate.

### 15.7 Character portraits

Characters should be recognizable across reappearances. Their appearance may change with age, travel, status, and circumstances.

Portraits should communicate personality and context without relying on stereotyped ethnic caricatures.

### 15.8 Music direction and soundtrack

The soundtrack should support:
- Distinct regional identities.
- Travel and discovery.
- Quiet domestic scenes.
- Markets and ports.
- Political tension.
- Religious and ceremonial settings.
- Loss and remembrance.
- The protagonist's recurring mystery.

Use historically informed instrumentation and musical research where feasible, while acknowledging that much ancient music cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

Sound design should make places feel inhabited: markets, workshops, harbors, river traffic, animals, weather, crowds, domestic life, food preparation, and music drifting from nearby spaces.

## 16. Progression and player development

Progression should reinforce the fantasy of becoming a more capable and connected traveler.

### 16.1 Progression dimensions

- Wealth and commercial capacity.
- Transport and route access.
- Language proficiency.
- Regional knowledge.
- Personal relationships.
- Faction standing.
- Reputation and recognition.
- Journal and historical discoveries.
- Access to new destinations and storylines.

### 16.2 No conventional class system

The player should not choose a fixed class such as warrior, merchant, priest, or diplomat. They are a merchant-traveler whose strengths emerge from their choices and experiences.

### 16.3 Perks

Perks should be modest, contextual, and narrative-facing.

Examples:
- Better negotiation with a particular class of merchant.
- Lower costs when arranging passage.
- Better understanding of local customs.
- Additional dialogue options in a court.
- Greater capacity to recognize a misleading offer.
- Easier access to certain caravan or maritime networks.
- Improved ability to secure lodging or hospitality.

Perks should generally emerge from experience, relationships, language, and discoveries rather than a large skill tree.

### 16.4 Avoiding grind

The player should not need to repeat identical actions to raise skills. Language, reputation, and relationships should progress through meaningful interactions and time spent in the world.

## 17. Endings and replayability

### 17.1 Multiple endings

There should be several substantial endings, determined by the player's journey and accumulated relationships rather than a final binary choice.

Relevant factors may include:
- Which regions the player visited.
- Which characters and families they helped.
- Which factions they supported or alienated.
- Which languages they learned.
- Whether they preserved important records or knowledge.
- Whether they established a lasting home or continued traveling.
- Which supernatural names and stories they pursued.
- Which regional historical arcs they witnessed.
- Which relationships survived the passage of time.

### 17.2 Regional ending variations

A player who spends much of the game in the eastern Mediterranean should receive a different final perspective from a player who travels deep into Nubia, South Asia, or China.

No single region should be mandatory for every ending. Some endings should require extensive travel or particular relationships, but the game should not punish a player for following a preferred route.

### 17.3 The protagonist's final choice

The final story should ask what the protagonist wants their extraordinary existence to mean.

Possible themes:
- To be remembered.
- To preserve what is being lost.
- To protect people rather than institutions.
- To belong somewhere.
- To continue moving.
- To accept or reject the divine identity others have given them.
- To let history forget them.

The game should not force a single interpretation of the protagonist's nature.

### 17.4 Post-ending play

After the main ending, the player should be able to continue traveling if the chosen ending supports it. The post-ending world should not pretend the central narrative has not concluded. It should reflect the ending and allow the player to revisit places, complete remaining personal stories, and explore.

- Language overview.

### 18.2 Interface principles

- Adjustable animation and screen effects.
- Save-anywhere or frequent autosave, subject to narrative consistency.
- A readable log of recent dialogue and events.

## 19. Save system and time

### 19.1 Save data

The save should preserve:
- Current date and narrative phase.
- Current location and travel status.
- Inventory, money, and transport.
- Known routes and destinations.
- Language proficiency.
- Character relationships and statuses.
- Faction relationships.
- Completed and pending events.
- Character arc progression.
- Migration and resettlement outcomes.
- Journal and discoveries.
- Random generator state, if random encounter selection is used.

### 19.2 Time advancement

Time advances through meaningful actions:
- Travel.
- Waiting for a ship or caravan.
- Certain contracts.
- Some local activities.
- Specific story choices.

The player should not be punished by a constantly ticking clock while browsing menus or reading dialogue.

Time should be used to support character arcs and historical progression, not to simulate every hour of the world.

### 19.3 Long chronology

The game must make the passage of years and decades legible:
- Display the current approximate date.
- Use chapter and regional timeline summaries.
- Show aging or generational changes in recurring characters.
- Let the journal record long gaps.
- Explain why a character or location has changed.
- Avoid surprising the player with an unexplained time jump.

## 20. Content and event architecture

This section describes the content model at a design level. It is not a mandate to build a complicated simulation engine.

### 20.1 Authored content first

The game should be driven by authored locations, characters, dialogue, events, and encounters. Systems should determine when authored content is eligible and how choices affect it.

No live LLM-generated dialogue or story content is required for the game.

### 20.2 Event record

An event may include:
- Unique identifier.
- Title and short description.
- Date or date range.
- Region and eligible locations.
- Historical confidence category.
- Source references for historical claims.
- Trigger conditions.
- Required characters or factions.
- Language or relationship conditions.
- Player choices.
- Consequences.
- Delayed follow-up events.
- Journal entry.
- Art and audio references.

### 20.3 Consequence types

Keep consequences explicit and limited to understandable categories:
- Change money or inventory.
- Advance time.
- Change a character relationship.
- Change a faction relationship.
- Change a character's location or status.
- Unlock or close a route.
- Change a local destination's narrative condition.
- Schedule a future event.
- Record a journal entry.
- Unlock a language or knowledge benefit.
- Resolve or advance a character arc.
- Record a migration or resettlement outcome.

### 20.4 Event selection

The event system should use eligibility and authored priority.

Examples:
- A specific historical event occurs in a defined date range.
- A refugee encounter becomes available after a nearby settlement is disrupted.
- A recurring character appears if they are alive, traveling, and plausibly on the route.
- A political checkpoint changes based on faction relationships.
- A language-specific conversation becomes available after the player reaches a proficiency threshold.

Random selection should be limited to suitable pools of minor encounters. Major narrative events should not depend on luck.

### 20.5 Deterministic behavior

For debugging and reliable saves:
- Use a seeded random generator.
- Save its state.
- Keep event eligibility and consequences testable.
- Avoid hidden side effects in UI components.
- Keep content separate from presentation and core rules.

## 21. Scope boundaries

The game should resist feature creep.

### Explicitly out of scope

- Grand strategy.
- Managing armies, cities, populations, or governments.
- Realistic economic simulation.
- Global supply-chain simulation.
- Physics-based sailing or land travel.
- Walkable 3D or 2D cities.
- Open-world action combat.
- Complex survival meters for hunger, thirst, fatigue, and disease.
- Detailed crafting trees.
- Procedurally generated civilizations.
- A universal pantheon or generic god-favor system.
- Mandatory combat.
- A rigid class system.
- Repetitive grinding for language or reputation.
- Live AI-generated story content.

### Only include a mechanic if it does at least one of the following

- Makes the journey more interesting.
- Gives the player a meaningful decision.
- Deepens a relationship.
- Makes a region or culture more distinctive.
- Creates a useful consequence.
- Supports the central mystery.
- Helps the player understand or remember the world and colors it with detail.
- Provides emotional impact and leaves an impression.

If it does none of these, it probably does not belong.

## 23. Quality standards

The game should meet the following standards.

### Narrative
- Important characters have motivations beyond serving the player.
- Recurring characters reappear for plausible reasons.
- Choices have consequences, including delayed consequences.
- The player can miss some stories without breaking the main narrative.
- Major events are not reduced to exposition dumps.
- The protagonist's mystery remains intriguing without becoming incoherent.

### Worldbuilding
- Regions have distinct material cultures and social structures.
- Ordinary life is represented alongside rulers and wars.
- Historical uncertainty is handled responsibly.
- South Asia, China, Nubia, and the Red Sea world receive substantive content.
- The world is not presented as culturally uniform or centered exclusively on the Mediterranean.
- Do not only consider what is in each city or hub or region, but also what is between them. The nature, the deserts, the harsh conditions, the flora and fauna. A calm moment to oneself to reflect, meditate, philosophize even.

### Gameplay
- Trading is understandable and satisfying without requiring optimization.
- Travel is the primary source of discovery.
- Language creates advantages but does not block basic progress.
- Faction relationships have clear practical consequences.
- Refugee and character arcs are meaningful without becoming management systems.
- The player can make progress through multiple styles of play.

### Presentation
- Art and music make destinations memorable.
- The UI supports a large cast and long chronology.
- The game communicates changes in time and place clearly.
- The player can understand what they can do and why an option matters.

## 24. Historical research and source discipline

The following sources are starting points for research, not an exhaustive bibliography. Historical content should be reviewed by region and date before final writing.

### Late Bronze Age crisis
- Knapp, A. Bernard, and Sturt W. Manning. “Crisis in Context: The End of the Late Bronze Age in the Eastern Mediterranean.” *American Journal of Archaeology* 120.1 (2016). [Article](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.3764/aja.120.1.0099)
- The crisis should be treated as a series of regionally varied transformations, not one universally accepted causal event.

### South Asia
- The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History. “South Asia, 2000–1000 B.C.” [Overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/03/ssa)
- Research should distinguish the post-urban Harappan regional worlds, early Vedic textual traditions, and archaeological cultures. Correlations between material cultures and textual identities must be treated cautiously.

### China
- Cambridge University Press, *The Shang Economy*, excerpt and chronology. [Excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97810095/65172/excerpt/9781009565172_excerpt.pdf)
- Keightley and other scholarship on Shang oracle-bone inscriptions and royal divination should inform the Anyang storyline.
- The Late Shang period and the transition to Zhou rule should be researched with attention to archaeological chronology and the distinction between contemporary evidence and later historical narratives.

### Nubia and Kush
- Bruce Beyer Williams, “Kush in the Wider World During the Kerma Period,” in *The Oxford Handbook of Ancient Nubia*. [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/35472/chapter-abstract/303801032)
- Research must distinguish Kerma-period political history from later Kushite states.

### Punt and the Red Sea
- John Baines, “Ancient Egypt – The Land of Punt,” Historical Association. [Overview](https://www.history.org.uk/podcasts/podcast/696/ancient-egypt-the-land-of-punt)
- “Adulis and the transshipment of baboons during classical antiquity,” *eLife*, including discussion of the uncertainty around Punt's location and the long history of Red Sea exchange. [Article](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.87513)

### Research requirements for production
For every major location, route, historical event, good, transport mode, ritual, and named historical figure:
1. Record the date range.
2. Record the relevant primary and secondary sources.
3. Note what is directly attested and what is reconstructed.
4. Note competing scholarly interpretations where they affect the story.
5. Avoid presenting uncertain geography, migration, or political boundaries as settled fact.
6. Have culturally specific material reviewed by someone with relevant expertise where possible.

## 25. Cultural-content production standard

The cultural richness of the game must be developed destination by destination, not added as generic flavor after the main systems are complete.

For each major city, settlement, port, court, or regional hub, create a **destination dossier** before finalizing its scenes. The dossier should cover:

1. **People:** A representative cast across social roles, including ordinary households, farmers, herders, fishers, craftspeople, merchants, performers, religious figures, officials, soldiers, and local elites as appropriate.
2. **Food:** Common ingredients, staple foods, meal preparation, drinks, market goods, preservation, hospitality, feasts, and seasonal variation.
3. **Music:** Instruments, performers, performance contexts, ritual and secular roles, and the limits of historical reconstruction.
4. **Stories:** Myths, legends, epics, fables, oral histories, proverbs, and the different people who tell them.
5. **Religion:** Local deities, sacred places, ritual practice, festivals, funerary customs, and the relationship between religious and political life.
6. **Language:** Languages and scripts, multilingual contacts, forms of address, interpreters, and language-learning opportunities.
7. **Daily life:** Work rhythms, household routines, markets, workshops, family life, recreation, and seasonal activity.
8. **Historical change:** What changes in this location over the game's chronology, what is directly supported, and what is fictional connective narrative.
9. **Recurring characters:** Who lives here, who visits, who may flee, who may return, and how local stories connect to wider character arcs.
10. **Sensory identity:** The sounds, colors, materials, smells, weather, landscape, animals, and visual details that distinguish this place.

The dossier should separate:
- Evidence directly attested for this place and period.
- Evidence known from a nearby region or a somewhat different period.
- Reasonable reconstruction.
- Fictional content created for the game.

Do not copy a cultural profile from one city to another merely because both belong to the same broad civilization. Cities within the same kingdom or cultural sphere can differ in dialect, food availability, local cults, political loyalties, trade, and social character.

### Mythological character research standard

For each deity, demigod, culture hero, or legendary figure considered for a character role:
- Identify the culture and period in which the tradition is attested.
- Record the relevant names, epithets, domains, stories, and iconography.
- Distinguish contemporary evidence from much later literary sources.
- Note whether the figure is local, regional, widely worshiped, or known primarily through later tradition.
- Avoid assuming that a later familiar version of a myth was already fixed in the game's period.
- Explain whether the game's portrayal is direct, adapted, speculative, or deliberately ambiguous.
- Preserve contradictions between sources and traditions where they are meaningful.

The protagonist's divine-traveler theme should act as a thread connecting the narrative, not as a reason to make every culture's mythology conform to one template.

### Historical figures and fictional interaction

Historical people can be characters, but their dramatic role must be grounded in what is known. The player may interact with them in fictional scenes, but the writing team must know which parts of the scene are attested, reconstructed, or invented. Do not attribute invented private conversations, secret motives, or supernatural identities to historical figures as if these were historical facts.

## 26. Final design statement

*The Song of the Wanderer* is about traveling through a world that is both familiar and strange, meeting people whose lives continue beyond the player's immediate attention, and discovering how history is experienced by those who must live through it.

The player begins with a small amount of money and a few contacts. Their first contact may even be someone who worships them. They learn how to travel, trade, speak, negotiate, and build trust. They come to know cities and communities. They become involved in the lives of merchants, families, rulers, religious figures, refugees, and ordinary people. They watch old networks fracture and new ones form. They follow people and stories across regions and generations, from the Mediterranean to the Nile, from Mesopotamia toward South Asia, and eventually to the Shang world of ancient China.

The protagonist may be more than human, but the heart of the game is human: what people remember, what they lose, what they carry with them, and what they build somewhere new. These are times of great change and turmoil and suffering. There will be ups and downs. The power of the human spirit, community, one's people, and the wonder of all the world can offer are very much central themes.

**The central question is not whether the player can change the course of history. It is what kind of mark one traveler can leave on the lives that history carries along through the power of the butterfly effect.**
