# Dossier — Ugarit

**Status:** slice destination, authored · **Confidence basis:** excavated city, attested
scripts and archives; every individual, price, and building-as-activity is invented.
**Time:** c. 1230 BCE. Nothing here uses the city's later destruction as foreshadowing.

## Why this place

Ugarit is the best-documented harbor on the northern Levantine coast for this period: a
palace archive, a scribal tradition in two scripts, and a merchant correspondence that
shows goods, ships, and obligations moving between the Levant, Cyprus, Anatolia, and
Egypt. It gives the slice a place where a stranger with a commodity is *legible*: there
are officials to satisfy, scribes to learn from, and households that remember a debt.

## The city as the slice uses it

- **The harbor** — moles, warehouses, porters, ships' masters. Passage is bought, worked,
  or owed. Work is arranged by morning, not by contract.
- **The gate and the palace** — an official writes names and cargo; a seal means more than
  a letter. The palace cares about dues, grain, and not being embarrassed.
- **The market** — mats and jars rather than a bazaar of shops. Grain, oil, wine, cloth,
  pottery, copper, tin, cedar, incense. Silver is weighed; there are no coins.
- **The temple** — a singer practicing a hymn; oil for lamps; a place where a name from an
  older story can be heard and misheard.
- **The workshop quarter** — weavers, potters, a household that has taken in relatives from
  inland. This is where the slice's refugee thread begins.

## Trade and money

- Value is weighed metal. A price is a number of units of silver or copper by weight, and
  the scale is part of every transaction.
- Grain and oil move in bulk; copper and tin are heavy and strategic; cedar is bulky and
  valuable; cloth and incense are light and worth carrying.
- Harbor dues exist and are resented. A palace seal can make a problem disappear; it is
  also a leash.
- **Units on screen.** Prices are **shekels of silver** and cargo is weighed in **minas**,
  the units this coast actually used. The local shekel was of the order of 9–10 g; a mina
  was 50 shekels and a talent 60 minas in the Syrian system (`documented` that these units
  were used and weighed, `plausible` for the exact local standard). The slice abstracts the
  quantities: one good is one unit of cargo and its weight is a play figure (a copper ingot
  4 minas, a jar of oil 3, a porter's load 20), and prices are authored for play rather than
  converted from a metrological table. No number on screen is a claim to a real price.
- **One purse.** The traveler's silver is the only balance kept. A price on either shore
  is silver by weight, the island's copper is cargo weighed in minas, and nothing converts
  at a border: no exchange rate, no second money (ADR-0009). The destination `currency`
  field describes what a place values, and is never read by a rule.

## Language and writing

- **Akkadian** in cuneiform is the language of diplomacy and long-distance commerce. A
  merchant who speaks some Akkadian can argue about a price instead of accepting it.
- **Ugaritic** is written in an alphabetic cuneiform of about thirty signs. Local speech,
  local songs, local paperwork.
- Scribes are professionals with their own interests. Lessons cost silver or favors.

## Names and gods (how the slice handles them)

The city's pantheon includes El, Baal, Anat, and others; the slice refers to "the lady of
the evening" rather than making a god a character. **Ninshubur** appears only as a name in
a hymn a trader brought from the north — a messenger's name out of older Sumerian and
Akkadian texts. Nobody in Ugarit identifies the traveler with it; the singer only notices
a resemblance she cannot explain. The theonym is `documented`; using it as the traveler's
story-name is fiction, and the resemblance is not an identification. See
`design/02-protagonist.md` §4.1 and ADR-0006.

## People (all invented)

Amnit (merchant household), Hannu (harbor official), Yada (scribe), Tiro (shipmaster),
Belet (temple singer), Sibtu (displaced weaver). Their roles are period-plausible; their
biographies are fiction. Briefs in `design/characters/slice-cast.md`.

## What the slice deliberately avoids

- No destruction, no siege, no "twilight of the Bronze Age" narration.
- No human sacrifice, no exoticised ritual.
- No claim that any character speaks for the city.
- No prices presented as data: they are game bands.
