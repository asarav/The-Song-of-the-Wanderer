# Cast threads — the slice

The recurring cast, what each of them wants, and how that want shows across scenes. This is
the reference for M4b-T01; dialogue is written from this page, not the other way round.

## The social grammar they live in

These are Late Bronze Age coastal people, not modern individuals with modern vocabulary.
The pressures that make them feel real are period pressures:

- **The household** is the unit of reputation. A household head who cannot feed dependants
  loses standing; a dependant who shames the house is a problem for everyone.
- **Patronage and obligation** bind people more tightly than liking. A favour is a debt with
  a name on it, and a debt unpaid is a public fact.
- **Hospitality** is sacred and calculating at once. Feeding a stranger creates a claim in
  both directions.
- **Shame and honour** are public. People do not say "I feel worthless"; they say what the
  quarter will say, or they change the subject.
- **The palace** is weather: distant, extractive, and impossible to ignore. Nobody trusts it;
  everybody needs its permission.
- **Bargaining** is a social performance with rules. Losing your temper is losing.
- **Death, hunger, childbirth and voyage** are near. Grief is loud in ritual and quiet in
  daily work; people do not perform stoicism, they keep working because the work must be
  done.
- **Religion** is not decoration. Oaths, offerings, omens and ancestors are part of how
  decisions are made.

Voice rules for this pass:

1. Each character wants something **in the scene**, not only across the game. They push.
2. Nobody explains their own psychology. Motive comes out through what they do with their
   hands, what they refuse to say, and what they ask in return.
3. When a character is indirect, there is a reason (fear of being overheard, shame, status,
   politeness, a bargain). Indirectness without a reason is the failure mode the playtest
   named.
4. When the stakes are high, they become direct. Anger, grief and affection are allowed.
5. No modern therapy language, no anachronistic egalitarianism, no knowing irony about the
   period.

## Threads the slice can pay off

- **Amnit** tests the player's honesty at the scale, makes Sibtu's problem her own, and on
  Cyprus needs a witness for a debt because her name is no good there. She never says she is
  helping Sibtu; the player can work it out from the cloth money.
- **Hannu** begins as a gate with a ledger and becomes a man being watched. The night watch
  shows him sleeping at the gate because the house is empty. The letter is him asking a
  stranger to carry a risk he cannot.
- **Yada** begins by insulting the player's hand and ends by admitting he has no one to
  leave his board to. His missing text is the reason he teaches at all.
- **Tiro** begins by complaining about passengers and ends by asking the player to carry a
  message to a widow on Cyprus. He never says the wreck was his fault.
- **Belet** begins with a name in a hymn and ends with the mundane explanation she is afraid
  of: the trader who taught her sister the name. The mystery stays open; the grief is real.
- **Sibtu** begins by refusing pity and ends by letting the player carry her cloth. The
  five shekels are not charity; they are a debt she intends to repay.
- **Kinyras** begins jovial and ends exact. His father's lost yard is why he counts twice.
- **Ada** begins by testing the tin and ends by teaching the girl in front of the player,
  which is the only compliment she gives.
- **Shipti** begins by pricing a room and ends by handing over a letter that might be about
  her brother. She charges for the room and not for the letter.

## Rules for later graphs

- A recurring character's second scene must answer the first one, not repeat it.
- A character may refuse to answer a question; the refusal must be legible as a choice.
- No one-time object, commission or night may repeat without a new reason.
- Emotional register changes with relationship: strangers get courtesy, familiars get
  bluntness, the trusted get silence.
