# People and ages

Source: design discussion, 2026-11-27. Claim category: **`plausible`** — a shape chosen
for a coherent world, not a measurement. No census survives for Late Bronze Age Cyprus,
Ugarit, or most of their neighbors, and none of the figures here should reach the player
as fact.

This document is authoring guidance. It adds no mechanic, no save field and no displayed
number. Its job is to stop the cast and the crowds from all being the same age.

## 1. What is known, and what is invented here

What the evidence can support:

- Settlement size and layout, house size, storage capacity and cemetery size give
  order-of-magnitude population estimates for a place, not counts of people.
- Cemetery and skeletal series give age-at-death distributions for the people buried in
  them. They are biased by who was buried, and by what survives in the soil.
- Food remains, imports, craft debris and household architecture show how many mouths a
  household fed and what work people did.
- Comparative premodern demography (model life tables) supplies the general shape of a
  high-fertility, high-mortality population.

What no source can support:

- A verified age-and-sex breakdown for a named settlement in 1230 BCE.
- A precise life expectancy for a region. Even the often-quoted 20–30 years at birth is a
  range built from model tables, not a measurement from one cemetery.
- Any claim that Ugarit, Cyprus, Egypt, Hatti, Mycenaean Greece and Mesopotamia had the
  same profile. They did not, and the differences matter for who is standing in a street.

One warning that shapes everything below: **life expectancy at birth is pulled down by
infant and child deaths**. A person who reached twenty could expect several decades more.
A low figure at birth does not mean a world of teenagers, and it does not mean a
fifty-year-old is exotic.

## 2. The baseline shape (design assumption)

Of every hundred living people, roughly:

| Band | Share | Who they are in a scene |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 | ~15 | carried, watched, asleep in a doorway; present, not speaking |
| 5–14 | ~22 | errands, water, charcoal, animals, a trade beginning; can speak and work |
| 15–29 | ~29 | apprentices, porters, sailors, new mothers, soldiers, junior scribes |
| 30–49 | ~24 | masters, householders, brokers, priests, smiths, ships' masters |
| 50+ | ~10 | few, and therefore memorable: memory-keepers, widows, retired masters |

So about **37 of every 100 are under fifteen**, and about 63 are adults. That is a young
population without being a village of children.

Among adults, assume a **slight surplus of women** — roughly 51:49 — because men are the
ones most often away at sea, in the mines, in a garrison, or dead of it. This is a small
lean, not a matriarchy, and the game should not comment on it. A mining camp or a fleet
town can invert it entirely.

## 3. Where the baseline bends

The useful distinction is between the population and **the people a traveler actually
meets**. A harbor, a mine, a garrison and a farming village do not show the same crowd.

| Settlement | Skew | Who the player sees |
|---|---|---|
| Harbor city (Ugarit) | mixed and mobile; young men over-represented on the quay, women and children in the houses behind it | foreign enclaves, porters and ships' boys in their teens and twenties, merchants and officials in their forties and fifties, a few old people in temple and palace service |
| Copper town (Cypriot harbor) | artisans' households, whole families, fewer transients than Ugarit | smiths, potters and their children in the workshop yard; women at the kiln and the scale; a broker in his forties |
| Mining camp | young men, few children, almost no old people; short careers | laborers fifteen to thirty, a handful of overseers in their forties, one or two specialists who stay |
| Garrison | young men, male-skewed, with a tail of older officers | soldiers in their late teens and twenties, a captain of thirty-five or fifty, local suppliers who are mostly women |
| Farming village | families; the most balanced and the oldest-skewed | children in number, parents in their thirties and forties, grandparents who are still working |
| Pastoral camp | mobile households; wide age range | boys and girls with animals, young men away with a herd, old women who remember routes and water |
| Palace or temple household | servants young, specialists middle-aged | trainee scribes and cup-bearers, a master of the archive, a singer in her thirties, one elder with rank |

Rule: before populating a place for the first time, decide its profile. Do not carry
Ugarit's crowd to Cyprus, or a village's crowd to a mine.

## 4. Rules for characters

- **A role is not an age.** Write two shipmasters, one twenty-eight and impatient, one
  fifty-five and unhurried. A scribe can be nineteen; a broker can be sixty.
- Every recurring location should hold **at least one person over fifty and at least one
  under twenty** who is not a child being carried.
- Women do not appear only as wives and mothers. They run workshops, keep inns, sing in
  temples and weigh copper; the small adult surplus is not a theme.
- Give ages through relationships and work — "my mother's brother", "when I was a girl",
  "thirty years on this quay" — not through dates of birth.
- Children are people with tasks and opinions, not props. The girl sorting charcoal by
  size has a job, a hierarchy and a view about it.
- Elders are scarce enough that meeting one is an event. Give them knowledge, authority
  or a grudge, not only frailty.
- Do not age a character up to make them wise, or down to make them lively. Decide what
  the life would have done to them by that age.

## 5. The slice's cast

The nine are invented; these ages are **briefs**, not data. The character schema has no
age field, no screen prints one, and a save never stores one.

| Character | Age | What the age does |
|---|---|---|
| Amnit, merchant | early 40s | Old enough to have survived a bad year, young enough to still sail. |
| Hannu, harbor official | late 50s | Near the end of a career; knows exactly what he will not see. |
| Yada, scribe | 60s | The archive is his lifetime. He teaches because he is not finished. |
| Tiro, shipmaster | mid 40s | Still strong, beginning to feel winters. |
| Belet, temple singer | early 30s | A trained voice and no seniority yet. |
| Sibtu, weaver | mid 30s | Several children, a household rebuilding, hands that know cloth. |
| Kinyras, copper broker | late 40s | His name is not yet in a mainland ledger. |
| Ada, smith | late 30s | A workshop owner in her prime, training a daughter of nine. |
| Shipti, innkeeper | mid 50s | Has heard everything twice and prices it accordingly. |

Three of the nine are over fifty — a third of the cast, against about a tenth of the
population. That is not an error. **Any cast over-samples people with standing**, because
those are the people a traveler is introduced to; a harbor official, an archive-keeper
and an innkeeper are survivors of their trades. The crowd around them should not follow
the same ratio.

## 6. Other regions, later

Keep one baseline and bend it locally. Author each region's profile in its dossier under
*People and social roles*, and do not reuse Ugarit's:

- **Egypt** — a dense Nile population, very young, with Delta harbors drawing migrants;
  household and temple estates hold people of every age.
- **Hatti and Anatolia** — upland and pastoral communities alongside palace towns; the
  mobile part of the population looks nothing like a city street.
- **Mycenaean settlements** — palatial service pulls young men and women into workshops
  and fleets; the palace records the workers, not the children.
- **Mesopotamian cities** — large, young, and fed by immigration; the old are visible in
  temple and scribal roles, not in the fields.
- **South Asia and China** — separate evidence, separate profiles. Do not derive them
  from the Mediterranean baseline.

## 7. An old storyteller (unclaimed seed)

A natural use of a scarce elder: a storyteller whose account of a distant place does not
quite fit the world the traveler knows. Her memory is the resource; her age is why it is
rare.

Caveat: any version in which she remembers a world the protagonist visited centuries
earlier touches the protagonist's nature, which `02-protagonist.md` deliberately leaves
unresolved. That is a mystery rule, not a demographic one. This seed stays here as a
question until that document admits it; it must not be authored into dialogue or events
first.

## 8. What this is not

- **Not a census.** No figure here is a measurement, and nothing in the codex may present
  it as one. A specific claim in game text needs its own source under `HISTORY.md` §2.
- **Not a simulation.** No births, deaths, aging or population counters exist, and none
  is planned for the slice. The model is a prior for writers.
- **Not a quota.** A scene is peopled by who would plausibly be there, not by the table.
- **Not a license to write everyone old.** The baseline is young; the cast is where
  elders earn their rarity.
