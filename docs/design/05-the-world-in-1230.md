# The world in 1230 BCE: places, landscapes and seasons

The menu and the prologue show places the journey does not sail to, so that Ugarit and
Cyprus are understood as two shores in a world of very different ones. This note records
what each place was in c. 1230 BCE, what its landscape and people were like, and how
confident the claim is. It is the source for `WORLD_WONDERS` and the prologue beats in
`src/ui/title.ts`, and for the art brief in `docs/ASSETS.md` §2.6.

**The world is not one desert.** The eastern Mediterranean and Near East held river
marshes, oak and pine woodland, olive terraces, irrigated barley, grassland and steppe,
as well as true desert. Every illustration must show the ecology of its own region; a
beige, sand-only palette across the set is a failure of research, not a style.

## 2. Seasons and colour

Mediterranean landscapes are seasonal. A place that is lush in March is golden in
August without having become a desert. Use this as the default palette guide; Egypt,
Mesopotamia, Anatolia and monsoon South Asia keep their own rhythms.

| Season | Appearance |
| --- | --- |
| Winter | Green hills, running streams, rain, muddy roads, cloud |
| Spring | Wildflowers, fresh grass, flowering trees, green fields |
| Early summer | Golden grain, green orchards, drying grass — the default for the slice |
| Late summer | Dusty roads, dry grass, harvested fields, low streams |
| Autumn | Harvest, first rains, returning green |

Colours to use: emerald and olive green, wildflower red and violet, damp earth, deep
blue and green water, reeds, silvery olive, dark pine, warm limestone, ochre and dusty
rose, slate and blue-grey mountains, snow on the highest peaks. Avoid a single brown-
yellow-beige register.

## 3. Climate stress is local

A dry phase is attested in the southern Levant from about 1250 to 1100 BCE, and there is
evidence for it in coastal Syria and Cyprus; its severity, extent and role in the
collapse are debated. Use it as local experience, never as a global change of scenery:
a run of poor harvests, a shepherd going farther for grazing, a spring growing
unreliable, grain prices rising in one market and not another, a family moving toward a
river valley, a timber crew working farther from home. Some places suffer, others stay
productive, and others receive the displaced.

## 4. Paint, plaster and colour

Finished buildings were not bare stone or mud brick. Plaster — lime or gypsum — gave a
smooth, repairable surface, and paint, glazed brick, carved relief and dyed textiles were
part of the architecture. The same rules as the landscape apply: each region has its own
palette and its own restraint, and wealth decides how much of it a wall carries.

| Region | Surface and colour |
| --- | --- |
| Egypt | White limestone covered in painted reliefs and hieroglyphs; blue, green, red, yellow, black and gold; painted columns, cornices and doorways; ceilings of dark blue with stars |
| Ugarit and the Levant | Pale lime plaster over mud brick, warm stone and timber; painted interiors, doorways and bands; dyed awnings, hangings and cushions |
| Cyprus | Dressed stone lower walls with plastered and painted mud brick above; red and mauve pigment at Enkomi; ordinary houses simpler, and figurative murals should not be assumed |
| Mycenaean Greece | White plaster with strong flat reds, blues, yellows and black; processions, animals, hunting and ceremony in palace rooms; painted floors and ceilings |
| Hittite Anatolia | Stone foundations, mud brick, timber and plaster; painted plaster fragments including polychrome, but far thinner evidence for extensive figurative murals than in Egypt or Mycenae — keep decoration architectural and restrained |
| Mesopotamia | Plastered and painted mud brick, coloured architectural elements and carved detail; the blue glazed-brick gate aesthetic of later Babylon must not appear |
| Elam | Brick and plaster with glazed and painted detail where evidenced, as on the ziggurat terraces |

**Pigments.** Red and yellow ochre and iron oxide; orpiment for yellow; Egyptian blue, a
manufactured pigment, and azurite; malachite and copper greens; charcoal and soot black;
lime, gypsum and chalk white. Browns, oranges, pinks and greys come from mixing. Purple
belongs to the Aegean repertoire (it is attested at Pylos) but is a rare, costly accent,
not an everyday building colour.

**Ordinary and elite.** An elite house, palace or temple can carry figurative scenes,
painted columns, patterned floors, carved furniture and valuable materials. An ordinary
house has plastered walls in white or earth tones, perhaps one painted band or doorframe,
wooden doors, reed mats, textiles and storage jars. Most surviving spectacle comes from
palaces, temples and tombs: do not give every home a mural.

**Ugarit.** Draw on the wider Syrian and northern Levantine tradition of decorated palace
interiors, but distinguish that regional parallel from what is actually excavated at
Ugarit. Painted plaster, coloured bands and decorated doorways are safe; a specific
figurative programme is not.

**Weathering.** Nothing is freshly painted everywhere. Some walls are newly whitewashed,
others faded, peeling, smoke-darkened, repaired or streaked by rain. A plain plastered
lower wall with a painted band and a more elaborate upper scene is a period-real scheme.

**Details that carry colour.** Painted timber doors with carved panels and metal fittings;
packed earth, plaster or stone floors; painted column shafts and capitals; roof beams,
reeds and mats; curtains, rugs and wall hangings; painted and inlaid chests, stools, beds
and tables.

  Hattusa, Ugarit, Enkomi, Chogha Zanbil and Dur-Kurigalzu; the caution about later

- Site identifications and dates follow the standard excavation literature for Karnak,
  Giza, Hattusa, Chogha Zanbil, Mycenae, Abu Simbel, Dur-Kurigalzu, Enkomi, Yinxu

  Mycenaean tholoi, or the foundation of Dur-Kurigalzu), the card says "at the court" or
  "already old" rather than a year.

## 6. What the cards may claim

The cards describe the place as it stood and the people who kept it, in two sentences.
They must not promise that the journey reaches it, and they must not print a population
figure, a life expectancy or a date that the sources do not support. Dates and numbers
live here, not in player-facing prose.
